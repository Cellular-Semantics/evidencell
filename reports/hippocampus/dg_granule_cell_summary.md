## Introduction

Dentate gyrus granule cells (GCs) are the principal excitatory neurons of the dentate gyrus and the input gate of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit. They receive glutamatergic input from layer II of the entorhinal cortex and project unmyelinated mossy fibers to CA3 pyramidal cells, with their somata densely packed in the dentate gyrus granule cell layer [UBERON:0005381] [1][2][3]. Mossy fiber terminals in CA3 stratum lucidum [UBERON:0014550] are a defining anatomical hallmark of this cell type [1]. Although the granule cell is unambiguously glutamatergic, its mossy fiber terminals carry GAD, the vesicular GABA transporter VIAAT, and AMPA + GABA<sub>A</sub> receptor machinery; juvenile co-release of GABA is a developmental feature that does not redefine the cell as non-glutamatergic [3]. Adult-born immature granule cells form a distinct functional subpopulation with delayed maturation, and dorsal vs. ventral heterogeneity within the granule layer is implied by the literature — both are potential sources of within-type structure that any atlas mapping must accommodate.

- **Soma location:** classical anatomy review · mouse/rat · [1]
  > The principal cells are interconnected by glutamatergic synapses, forming the "trisynaptic pathway" (Andersen et al., 1971)(Storm- Mathisen, 1977). The GCs of the dentate gyrus receive excitatory glutamatergic input from layer II pyramidal cells of the entorhinal cortex (Steward et al., 1976)) and project to CA3 pyramidal cells. From there, they project to CA1 cells, which in turn project to the subiculum and back to the entorhinal cortex (Andersen et al., 1971)(Amaral et al., 1990). The GCs show highly conserved properties across species (Seress et al., 1990) and are born continuously throughout life (Altman et al., 1990), a feature that may be related to their role in memory formation (Schmidt-Hieber et al., 2004). There are approximately one million GCs within the rat dentate gyrus, all projecting thin unmyelinated axons into the stratum lucidum of the CA3, adjacent to the cell body layer.
  > — Munster-Wandowski et al. 2013, Major Glutamatergic Cell Types in Hippocampal Subfields · [1] <!-- quote_key: 7458943_efa15be5 -->

- **Soma location / circuit context:** DG principal-cell review · [2]
  > The circuitry of the dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampus is unique compared to other hippocampal subfields because there are two glutamatergic principal cells instead of one: granule cells, which are the vast majority of the cells in the DG, and the so-called "mossy cells."
  > — Scharfman & Myers 2013, Mossy Cells: Specialized Glutamatergic Neurons · [2] <!-- quote_key: 11290620_48a528f1 -->

- **Neurotransmitter (glutamatergic with developmental GABA co-release):** mossy fiber terminal review · [3]
  > Granule cells (GCs) in the dentate gyrus are crucial for transferring information from the entorhinal cortex to the hippocampus proper where they integrate the classical excitatory trisynaptic circuit (McBain, 2008). Although primarily glutamatergic, the axons of GCs, the mossy fibers (MFs), contain GABA, its synthesizing enzyme glutamic acid decarboxylase (Schwarzer et al., 1995)(Sloviter et al., 1996) and the vesicular GABA transporter VIAAT (Zander et al., 2010). In addition, immunogold experiments have demonstrated the presence of both AMPA and GABA A receptors, co-localized on MF terminals in close spatial relation with synaptic vesicles (Bergersen et al., 2003). All these pieces of evidence suggest that MF-cornu ammon (CA3) synapses can use GABA as a neurotransmitter since they posses all the machinery for synthesizing, storing, releasing, and sensing it
  > — Pedroni et al. 2014, Developmental and Temporal Characteristics · [3] <!-- quote_key: 11333153_7e2a7ff8 -->
