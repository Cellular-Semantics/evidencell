## Identity as a novel CA1 excitatory projection neuron

The CA1 radiatum giant cell (RGC) is characterised across the corpus as a distinct excitatory projection neuron of the hippocampus, separate from CA1 pyramidal cells. The Introduction of Kirson & Yaari 2000 reviews prior evidence that RGCs project out of the hippocampus (originally established by Gulyas et al. 1998), with spiny dendrites, a myelinated axon, and asymmetric (excitatory) synaptic contacts — properties typical of glutamatergic projection neurons rather than interneurons [1].

> "it has spiny dendrites and a myelinated axon. Second, its axon collaterals in stratum oriens form asymmetric (presumably excitatory) synapses (Gulyas et al., 1998). Third, it manifests a prominent spike afterdepolarization (ADP) and displays spike frequency accommodation (Maccaferri and McBain, 1996)." [1]

> "RGCs most likely represent a novel type of excitatory hippocampal projection neuron (Gulyas et al., 1998)." [1]

The abstract frames the RGC explicitly as "a recently discovered excitatory projection neuron" being functionally assessed [1].

> "We used the whole-cell patch-clamp technique in adult hippocampal slices to assess the role of NMDA receptors in synaptic excitation of a recently discovered excitatory projection neuron, the CA1 radiatum giant cell (RGC)." [1]

## Morphology and anatomical location

The morphological characterisation of RGCs is described in a dedicated Results section ("Morphological characterization of RGCs") and in the Materials and Methods identification criteria. RGCs have large triangular somata located in stratum radiatum, two apical dendrites ascending toward stratum lacunosum-moleculare, and an axon that traverses stratum pyramidale, bifurcates in stratum oriens, and projects in opposite directions toward the subiculum and fimbria [1].

> "the photomicrograph of a typical RGC ( Fig. 1 B) shows that its large, triangular cell body is located in the stratum radiatum. Its two apical dendrites ascend toward the stratum lacunosum-moleculare and arborize extensively." [1]

> "The axon of the RGC arises vertically, traversing the stratum pyramidale and bifurcating in the stratum oriens into two main branches, which extend in opposite directions toward the subiculum and the fimbria" [1]

The Methods criteria reinforce these features as identification-grade morphological diagnostics:

> "The RGC s were identified by their location in stratum radiatum, the unusually large dimensions of their somata and the one to two prominent dendrites directed toward the stratum lacunosum-moleculare." [1]

Three morphological subtypes are documented in the Results (symmetrical two-dendrite form; one main plus one shorter parallel dendrite; pyramidal cell-like single bifurcating apical dendrite), consistent with heterogeneity within this rare cell class.

## NMDA receptor-dependent burst firing — the defining electrophysiology

Across multiple Results and Discussion sections, RGCs are repeatedly distinguished from pyramidal cells (PCs) by NMDA-dependent burst firing. Afferent stimulation evokes at most one spike in PCs but readily evokes a burst in RGCs; the burst persists after non-NMDA receptor blockade and is abolished by NMDA receptor block [1].

> "Glutamatergic synaptic activation, even after blocking non-NMDA receptors, fired an NMDA receptor-dependent burst of action potentials in RGCs." [1]

> "Whereas afferent fiber stimulation evokes maximally one action potential in PCs, the same stimulation readily evokes a spike burst in RGCs." [1]

> "Furthermore, the burst response persists after blockade of non-NMDA receptors but is sensitive to blocking NMDA receptors, indicating an enhanced NMDA EPSC component in these neurons." [1]

APV abolished the burst while CNQX did not:

> "APV completely suppressed the burst response that followed the first evoked spike" [1]

> "After blocking non-NMDA receptors with CNQX, RGCs still fired a" [1]

> "NMDA receptors contribute to synaptic recruitment of RGCs whether GABA A-ergic inhibition is operative or not." [1]

Quantitatively, RGC EPSCs are at least threefold larger than PC EPSCs, and large EPSPs persist after non-NMDA receptor blockade — direct evidence that NMDA receptors are recruited at resting membrane potential [1].

> "Even when activated by the same population of afferent fibers, the amplitude of the compound EPSCs was at least threefold greater in RGCs than in PCs at all stimulus intensities." [1]

> "large EPSPs could be evoked in RGCs even after blockage of non-NMDA receptors, indicating that a substantial fraction of these receptors are not blocked at resting membrane potential." [1]

The Concluding remarks frame RGCs as a novel functional class [1]:

> "Three factors were found to contribute to the enhanced synaptic excitability of RGCs versus PCs, namely, much larger EPSCs, decreased blockade of synaptic NMDA receptors by Mg2+, and prolonged decay of NMDA EPSCs." [1]

> "RGCs are the first hippocampal neurons shown to manifest this exceptional form of synaptic recruitment." [1]

## NMDA receptor biophysics: reduced Mg2+ block and slow kinetics

Mechanistic Results sections quantify the unusual NMDA receptor biophysics. Mg2+ block is shifted to more negative potentials and has lower affinity in RGCs [1].

> "the region of negative slope conductance in the I-V relation started 12 mV more negative in the RGCs (−39 mV) than in the PCs (−27 mV)" [1]

> "the affinity of NMDA receptors for Mg2+ at resting membrane potential (approximately −60 mV) was significantly higher in PCs than in RGCs (Kd(-60 mV), 0.15 ± 0.05 vs. 0.42 ± 0.07 mM, respectively)" [1]

> "in RGCs, the Mg2+ block of NMDA receptors is less voltage-dependent than in PCs (δ 0.80 vs 0.95, respectively)" [1]

Decay kinetics are characteristically slower in RGCs, with a smaller fast/slow amplitude ratio and faster rise times — confirmed by space-clamp control experiments [1].

> "The rise times of NMDA EPSCs were significantly faster in RGCs than in PCs (9.6 ± 0.7 msec in RGCs vs 11.7 ± 0.6 msec in PCs)" [1]

> "NMDA EPSCs in RGCs have faster rise times and slower decay kinetics than in PCs" [1]

> "the relative contribution of Af compared with As was much smaller in the RGC (Af/As = 2.25) than in the PC (Af/As = 6.2)" [1]

> "The slower decay kinetics of NMDA receptors in RGCs, by causing a greater charge transfer through the receptor channels, would contribute to the sustained synaptic activation of these neurons. It also would lead to an increased calcium signal in these neurons, which may be important for their ability to express LTP" [1]

## Candidate molecular basis: NR2D subunit

The Discussion section "Implications for subunit composition of NMDA receptors in RGCs" links the biophysical signature (reduced Mg2+ block, slow kinetics) to a hypothesised NR2D subunit contribution, distinguishing RGCs from adult CA1 PCs, which express only NR2A and NR2B [1]. This is Discussion-level inference rather than direct molecular detection.

> "Several studies have shown that adult CA1 PCs express only the NR2A and NR2B subunits" [1]

> "A likely candidate would be the NR2D subunit, given its low Mg2+ sensitivity and slow offset kinetics (Monyer et al., 1994)." [1]

This NR2D (GRIN2D) hypothesis is suggestive but does not yet constitute a confirmed defining marker — it is an inference from pharmacology, not from transcriptomic or immunohistochemical data in RGCs.
